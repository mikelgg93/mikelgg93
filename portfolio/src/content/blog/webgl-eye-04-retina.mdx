We have shaped the [cornea](/blog/webgl-eye-01-cornea), opened the [pupil](/blog/webgl-eye-02-pupil), and given the <PublishedPost id="webgl-eye-03-lens" fallback="lens"><a href="/blog/webgl-eye-03-lens/">lens</a></PublishedPost> a way to change focus. There is now somewhere for light to go, but nobody to receive it.

Enter the **retina**: a curved sheet of neural tissue lining the back of the eye. Calling it the eye's camera sensor is a useful first approximation. It is also a trap. The retina does not simply record an image and send the pixels onwards. Its cells begin processing visual information before the signals leave the eye.

As before, we'll build the easy version first. One surface, then layers, then repeated cells. Only after that will we ask what happens when a continuous image meets a finite collection of photoreceptors.

## First approximation: A dome

Our first retina is a cup. We sample angles around a sphere and retain its posterior portion. It is the opposite end of the construction we used for the cornea: this time we look into the concave surface.

For this visualisation, choose a radius of $10.8\ \text{mm}$. This is a **schematic dimension**, not a universal retinal curvature. MRI measurements show that eye dimensions and shape vary, including with refractive error (Atchison et al., 2004). A spherical cup is a convenient starting mesh.

```ts
const theta = u * Math.PI * 0.49;
const r = radius * Math.sin(theta);
const x = r * Math.cos(angle);
const y = r * Math.sin(angle);
const z = centreZ - radius * Math.cos(theta);
```

Here, `u` runs from 0 at the posterior pole to 1 at the rim; `angle` runs from 0 to 2π around the axis. Coordinates are in millimetres, with positive z towards the cornea. The angle stops just short of a hemisphere. The dome is already formed; turn off **Mesh guide** to inspect the surface.

The warm tint makes the surface visible; it is not a prediction of fundus colour. This shell gives us position and curvature. It has not yet given us a single light-sensitive cell.

## Material and landmarks

We are looking at the surface from inside the eye. The **fovea** is the small depression at the centre of the macula; the **retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)** lies behind the photoreceptors. The nerve leaves through the disc on the nasal side, passing through the eye’s outer wall, the **sclera**.

The next sequence adds material, foveal relief, major vessels and the optic nerve exit. Colour helps us read the surface; it does not simulate a fundus photograph. The pit changes the mesh, and the vessels avoid the foveal centre. This is animated construction, not tissue growth.

The material uses a rough, nonmetallic surface and vertex colours: the colours identify regions, while the normals control how they respond to light. We are not calculating spectral scattering in tissue.

```ts
// One linear-RGB triplet per vertex; chosen colours, not measured reflectance.
geometry.setAttribute("color", new THREE.Float32BufferAttribute(colours, 3));
const material = new THREE.MeshStandardMaterial({
  vertexColors: true,
  roughness: 0.78,
});
```

For the pit, move the inner surface outwards towards the RPE while keeping the outer boundary fixed. A Gaussian confines the change near the centre; then recompute the normals.

```ts
const innerRadius = CUP_RADIUS
  + PIT_DEPTH * Math.exp(-((distance / PIT_WIDTH) ** 2));
```

`distance` measures surface distance from the foveal axis. The magnified profile keeps equal scales on both axes, exposing the pit without changing the eye geometry. We precompute the starting and final positions and normals; the GPU interpolates between them.

The vessels are curves on that same surface, swept into tubes that taper towards their ends. The nerve occupies the nasal opening and bends outside the sclera. The sequence plays once and holds the result; **Replay** starts it again.

## Turn the surface into a tissue

Light arrives from the **vitreous**, the gel-filled space behind the lens. In the vertebrate retina, rods and cones lie towards the outer side, close to the **retinal pigment epithelium**, or RPE. Light generally crosses the inner neural layers before reaching their light-sensitive outer segments.

The RPE supports photoreceptors: among its jobs are absorbing stray light, participating in the visual cycle, and phagocytosing shed outer-segment material. Behind it lies the vascular **choroid**. The neural retina and its pigment epithelium are closely associated, but they are not interchangeable names for one layer (Kolb, *Simple Anatomy of the Retina*).

The section below groups the tissue into seven readable bands. **Nuclear layers** contain cell bodies; **plexiform layers** contain many of the synaptic connections. It is a map of the main compartments, not the complete conventional layer count. The nerve-fibre and ganglion-cell layers are grouped together, and the external limiting boundary is represented schematically.

## A pit with a purpose: The fovea

Now turn on **Show fovea**. The inner layers thin out towards the centre while the outer bands remain. In a typical fovea, inner retinal tissue is displaced away from the centre; tightly packed central cones become narrow and elongated. The central region is also free of retinal blood vessels; its outer retina is supplied by the choroid, so “avascular” does not mean without a blood supply. The pit, cone specialisation and vascular arrangement are related features, rather than a hole punched through the retina (Provis et al., 2013).

Our profile captures that change in organisation, with exaggerated vertical dimensions. One highlighted cone route suggests lateral displacement; it does not reconstruct individual Henle fibres or measured layer thicknesses. Nor does pit depth alone determine visual acuity: central cone specialisation can occur even when a conventional pit is absent.

**OCT** gives us a way to inspect retinal depth in a living eye. It uses [low-coherence interferometry](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1957169/) to resolve backscattered light with depth. A cross-sectional B-scan often makes the foveal contour immediately recognisable. But an OCT image is not a photograph of seven painted sheets. Bright and dark bands depend on tissue scattering and the imaging system, and their anatomical interpretation requires care. Spaide and Curcio (2011), for example, examined how outer-retinal OCT bands correspond to photoreceptor structures.

The diagram above is an anatomical sketch, **not a simulated OCT scan**. Its colours label compartments; its brightness says nothing about OCT reflectivity.

## Zoom into a receptor patch: Rods and cones

A real retina contains far too many photoreceptors to draw each one in a lightweight browser demo. In their histological sample, Curcio et al. (1990) reported averages of roughly **4.6 million cones and 92 million rods per retina**, with substantial variation between eyes. These are measured study averages, not a fixed specification for every human eye.

The distribution matters as much as the totals. Cone density peaks sharply near the foveal centre and falls away from it. Rods are absent from the central rod-free zone and abundant outside it; their density also varies across the retina. The optic disc, where ganglion-cell axons leave the eye, has no rods or cones and corresponds to the physiological blind spot.

We will draw a small set of **enlarged photoreceptor icons**. A cone and a rod each need one geometry and material; `InstancedMesh` repeats them at different positions with relatively few draw calls.

```ts
const cells = new THREE.InstancedMesh(geometry, material, capacity);
transform.position.set(x, y, z);
transform.updateMatrix();
cells.setMatrixAt(index, transform.matrix);
cells.instanceMatrix.needsUpdate = true;
```

Rods support very sensitive vision in dim conditions. Cones support daylight vision and colour discrimination. The S-, M- and L-cone classes have broad, overlapping spectral sensitivities: they are not red, green and blue screen pixels arranged in repeating triplets (Stockman & Sharpe, 2000). Our gold colour identifies *cones as a group*; the demo does not assign cone classes or simulate colour vision.

## Sampling: More detail is not always more information

Imagine projecting fine stripes onto a row of receptors. If the stripes become finer than that row can distinguish, two different patterns can produce the same samples. This is **aliasing**: detail can become ambiguous, rather than simply disappearing into a blur.

To isolate the idea, leave the anatomy aside for a moment. The next demo uses equally spaced **point samples in one dimension**. Frequency is measured in cycles across the panel, not cycles per degree of visual angle. With $N$ samples across that interval, its Nyquist boundary is $N/2$ cycles per interval. At the boundary itself, phase can make the signal disappear from the samples; unique reconstruction requires appropriate band-limiting below it.

Start with 16 samples and increase the stripes beyond 8 cycles. The bottom row shows a lower-frequency pattern that agrees at the sample positions. Add more samples and the boundary moves. **Shift ¼ cycle** changes where the stripes fall relative to the receptors; at certain settings, every sample can land on the same grey.

This is a sampling experiment, not a retinal-image or perception simulator. Receptors have finite collecting areas, ocular optics filter the incoming pattern, and neural circuitry, adaptation and eye movements also matter. Human foveal aliasing has been demonstrated experimentally under carefully controlled conditions (Williams, 1985); our regular one-dimensional row is a way to understand the ambiguity, not a way to calculate somebody's visual acuity.

## The retina is already doing computation

An absorbed photon initiates a biochemical cascade. In rods and cones, the light response **hyperpolarises** the cell and reduces glutamate release. That change is the beginning of a neural message, not a tiny electrical photograph.

Bipolar cells pass signals towards ganglion cells; horizontal and amacrine cells contribute lateral interactions and other processing. ON and OFF pathways respond differently to light increments and decrements. Ganglion-cell axons form the optic nerve, carrying action potentials onwards. The real circuitry includes multiple cell types and parallel routes, not a single cone-to-wire connection (Kolb, *How the Retina Works*).

There is also a link back to Part 2. Melanopsin-expressing **ipRGCs** are ganglion cells: they can respond intrinsically to light and also receive rod/cone input. The retina's contribution to the pupil light reflex therefore is not confined to one kind of photoreceptor.

Our meshes show where some of these structures belong. They do not implement phototransduction, receptive fields or spike trains. That is a different layer of modelling, and it deserves more than an animated arrow.

## Put the receiving surface behind the optics

We can now place the cup behind the cornea, iris and lens from the earlier articles. Their coordinate systems must agree. Here are the dimensions of the assembled, relaxed model, in millimetres:

| Measurement | Endpoints in this model | Value |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Central corneal thickness (CCT) | Anterior to posterior cornea, on axis | 0.55 |
| Internal anterior chamber depth (ACD) | Posterior cornea to anterior lens | 3.35 |
| Lens thickness (LT) | Anterior to posterior lens | 4.00 |
| Axial length (AXL) | Anterior cornea to the boundary representing the RPE | 24.30 |

The outer envelope is approximately **24.4 mm wide and high**, and **25.4 mm from the corneal apex to the outer posterior sclera**. That last distance is not the AXL in the table, which ends at the RPE. The 10.8 mm retinal radius and centre at $z=-7.6\ \text{mm}$ locate this envelope in the same coordinate system as the anterior segment. The anterior inner wall follows the same attachment profile as the ciliary body. The coats share their boundaries and the retina tapers to a schematic ora serrata behind the ciliary body.

These are **chosen dimensions**, not measurements from a person or a validated schematic optical eye. We retain the anterior parts from Part 3; we have not fitted their shape and spacing to a common biometric dataset. Optical biometry commonly defines axial length to the RPE, so that is the endpoint used here (Ostrin et al., 2023). The corneal apex is at $z=5.6\ \text{mm}$ and our RPE boundary at $z=-18.70\ \text{mm}$. The distance to the inner retina is different. We use **internal** ACD, excluding the corneal thickness.

The receiving surface needs a supporting wall. Outside the retina and RPE sits the **choroid**, a vascular coat that supplies the outer retina, including the photoreceptors. The retinal circulation supplies much of the inner retina (Kolb). Outside that is the **sclera**, the opaque, collagen-rich coat that gives the globe mechanical support and meets the cornea at the limbus (Watson & Young, 2004).

The **optic nerve** is the exit route. Ganglion-cell axons converge at the **optic disc**, nasal to the fovea, then pass through the scleral canal. The disc has no rods or cones: this is the anatomical basis of the blind spot. It is not the fovea, and the nerve should not emerge from the centre of our foveal drawing.

For the model, build nested walls with a shared, offset opening and attach a short nerve segment. Each wall has an inner surface, an outer surface and edges joining them. Their triangle winding must face out of the tissue on both sides: into the vitreous on the inner surface, away from it on the outer surface. Inside the canal, the nerve follows the same axis and opening radius; its bend begins outside the sclera. We cache both the closed and sectioned meshes, including the cut faces, so switching views does not rebuild geometry.

The **macula** surrounds the fovea; a roughly $5.5\ \text{mm}$ diameter is a common clinical description, with boundaries depending on the definition (Webvision, *The Architecture of the Human Fovea*). For this drawing we mark that region with a muted tint. We deform the inner surface with a shallow Gaussian depression for the **foveal pit**, leaving the outer RPE boundary in place. Its $0.10\ \text{mm}$ depth is a model parameter, not a universal foveal measurement. At whole-eye scale this should look subtle: the earlier layer view was magnified.

The major **retinal vessels** leave the disc and curve above and below the central macula, avoiding the foveal centre (Kolb; Provis et al., 2013). We sketch those routes as curves, then build small tubes whose centres follow the same retinal surface. Their arrangement and visible calibres are stylised; the capillary network and the exact foveal avascular-zone boundary are not modelled.

For this visualisation, wall thicknesses, colours, the disc offset and the short nerve segment are drawing parameters. The scleral rim meets the earlier corneal mesh; the anterior joins are schematic. We omit capillaries, the detailed lamina cribrosa, individual axons and meningeal layers. The layer diagram and receptor mosaic remain separate magnified views, not millimetre-sized cells added to this eye.

We now have the main anatomical pieces of an eye model. Putting them in one scene does not yet make a complete optical or neural simulation. To predict a retinal image we would have to couple and validate the surfaces, refractive indices and distances; to predict vision we would need to model what the retina and brain do with that image too.

For now, the construction has reached a useful milestone: light has a surface to reach, cells to sample it, and a reason that “more polygons” and “more visual information” are different things.
