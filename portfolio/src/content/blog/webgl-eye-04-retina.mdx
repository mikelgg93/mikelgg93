We have shaped the [cornea](/blog/webgl-eye-01-cornea), opened the [pupil](/blog/webgl-eye-02-pupil), and given the <PublishedPost id="webgl-eye-03-lens" fallback="lens"><a href="/blog/webgl-eye-03-lens/">lens</a></PublishedPost> a way to change focus. Light can now travel through our model, but we still need something to receive it.

That is the job of the **retina**, a curved sheet of neural tissue lining the back of the eye. Thinking of it as a camera sensor is a useful starting point, provided we remember that its cells do more than record an image: they begin processing visual information before the signals leave the eye.

As before, we'll start with one surface, then add layers and repeated cells until we can explore what happens when a continuous image reaches a finite collection of photoreceptors.

## First approximation: A dome

We can make our first retina by sampling angles around a sphere and keeping its posterior portion as a cup. This uses the opposite end of the construction from the cornea, with the view now looking into the concave surface.

For this visualisation, we choose a radius of $10.8\ \text{mm}$ as a **schematic dimension**. A spherical cup makes a convenient starting mesh, although real eye dimensions and shape vary, including with refractive error, as MRI measurements show (Atchison et al., 2004).

```ts
const theta = u * Math.PI * 0.49;
const r = radius * Math.sin(theta);
const x = r * Math.cos(angle);
const y = r * Math.sin(angle);
const z = centreZ - radius * Math.cos(theta);
```

Here, `u` runs from 0 at the posterior pole to 1 at the rim, while `angle` runs from 0 to 2π around the axis. Coordinates are in millimetres, with positive z towards the cornea and the cup stopping just short of a hemisphere. The dome is already formed, so you can turn off **Mesh guide** to inspect its surface.

The warm tint helps us see the surface without predicting fundus colour. We now have position and curvature, but the shell still needs the landmarks and cells that make it a retina.

## Material and landmarks

From inside the eye, we would see the **fovea**, a small depression at the centre of the **macula**, with the **retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)** behind the photoreceptors. Towards the nose, the nerve leaves through the optic disc and passes through the eye's outer wall, the **sclera**.

We add these landmarks in sequence: material, foveal relief, major vessels and the optic nerve exit. The palette follows the orange-red fundus often seen through an ophthalmoscope, with a darker macula and paler disc, although pigmentation and illumination affect its appearance (Clinical Methods, *The Funduscopic Examination*). These colours help identify regions rather than calibrating a fundus photograph or suggesting that neural retinal tissue is opaque orange. As the pit deforms the mesh, the vessels take routes around its centre; the animation shows construction, not tissue growth.

A rough, nonmetallic material gives the surface its appearance, using vertex colours to distinguish regions and normals to control the lighting response. This lets us read the anatomy without calculating spectral scattering in tissue.

```ts
// One linear-RGB triplet per vertex; chosen colours, not measured reflectance.
geometry.setAttribute("color", new THREE.Float32BufferAttribute(colours, 3));
const material = new THREE.MeshStandardMaterial({
  vertexColors: true,
  roughness: 0.9,
});
```

To form the pit, we move the inner surface outwards towards the RPE while keeping the outer boundary fixed. A Gaussian confines the displacement near the centre, after which we recompute the normals.

```ts
const innerRadius = CUP_RADIUS
  + PIT_DEPTH * Math.exp(-((distance / PIT_WIDTH) ** 2));
```

Here, `distance` measures surface distance from the foveal axis. A magnified profile with equal scales on both axes reveals the pit without changing the eye geometry, while the GPU animates its formation by interpolating between precomputed starting and final positions and normals.

For each vessel, we start with a curve in the x–y plane, project it onto the retinal surface and sweep a tube along the result:

```ts
// RetinalVesselCurve projects each curve sample onto retinalPoint(x, y).
const curve = new RetinalVesselCurve(points, radius + 0.008);
const vessel = new THREE.Mesh(
  new THREE.TubeGeometry(curve, 80, radius, 6, false),
  material,
);
```

As the tubes taper below a pixel in width, a thin screen-space stroke keeps their routes legible; its width is a drawing aid rather than a measured calibre. The nerve follows the nasal opening and bends outside the sclera. Once the sequence finishes, it holds the result and the magnified profile remains available, including with reduced motion. Use **Replay** to watch the construction again.

## Turn the surface into a tissue

To turn this surface into tissue, we need to follow light arriving from the **vitreous**, the gel-filled space behind the lens. In the vertebrate retina, rods and cones sit towards the outer side near the **retinal pigment epithelium**, or RPE, so light generally crosses the inner neural layers before reaching their light-sensitive outer segments.

The RPE supports these photoreceptors by absorbing stray light, participating in the visual cycle and phagocytosing shed outer-segment material, with the vascular **choroid** lying behind it. Although closely associated, the neural retina and pigment epithelium are distinct tissues (Kolb, *Simple Anatomy of the Retina*).

The section below maps the main compartments into seven readable bands, with cell bodies in the **nuclear layers** and many synaptic connections in the **plexiform layers**. This groups the nerve-fibre and ganglion-cell layers together and represents the external limiting boundary schematically, rather than reproducing the complete conventional layer count.

## A pit with a purpose: The fovea

Turning on **Show fovea** reveals how this organisation changes at the centre: the inner layers thin while the outer bands remain. In a typical fovea, inner retinal tissue is displaced sideways and tightly packed central cones become narrow and elongated. The centre is free of retinal vessels, but its outer retina receives blood from the choroid, so “avascular” does not mean without a blood supply. Together, these features form a specialised region rather than a hole through the retina (Provis et al., 2013).

Our profile makes this arrangement visible by exaggerating vertical dimensions, with one highlighted cone route suggesting lateral displacement. It does not reconstruct individual Henle fibres or measured layer thicknesses, and pit depth alone does not determine acuity: central cone specialisation can occur even without a conventional pit.

We can inspect this depth structure in a living eye with **OCT**, which uses [low-coherence interferometry](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1957169/) to resolve backscattered light with depth. A B-scan often reveals the foveal contour clearly, although its bright and dark bands depend on tissue scattering and the imaging system rather than resembling painted sheets. Interpreting those bands anatomically requires care, as Spaide and Curcio (2011) explored when relating outer-retinal OCT bands to photoreceptor structures.

Our diagram instead uses colours to label anatomical compartments, so it is **not a simulated OCT scan** and its brightness carries no information about OCT reflectivity.

## Zoom into a receptor patch: Rods and cones

At the cellular scale, a whole retina is too large for us to draw every photoreceptor in a lightweight browser demo. Curcio et al. (1990) reported averages of roughly **4.6 million cones and 92 million rods per retina** in their histological sample, with substantial variation between eyes rather than a fixed count for every person.

Where these cells sit matters as much as their total number. Cone density peaks sharply near the foveal centre and falls away from it, while rods are absent from the central rod-free zone and abundant beyond it, with density varying across the retina. Neither occurs at the optic disc, where ganglion-cell axons leave the eye, giving us the physiological blind spot.

We can explore this distribution with a small set of **enlarged photoreceptor icons**, giving a cone and a rod one geometry and material each. `InstancedMesh` then repeats them at different positions with relatively few draw calls:

```ts
const cells = new THREE.InstancedMesh(geometry, material, capacity);
transform.position.set(x, y, z);
transform.updateMatrix();
cells.setMatrixAt(index, transform.matrix);
cells.instanceMatrix.needsUpdate = true;
```

Viewed from above, the two equal-area patches show a denser, cone-only **Foveal centre** beside an **Away from centre** patch mixing cones and rods. **Show cell shapes** tilts both to reveal the narrower, longer central cones. We magnify these local patches separately from the whole-eye shell and use illustrative spacing rather than a measured density map.

The cell types also differ in function: rods support very sensitive vision in dim conditions, while cones support daylight vision and colour discrimination. S-, M- and L-cones have broad, overlapping spectral sensitivities rather than behaving like red, green and blue screen pixels in repeating triplets (Stockman & Sharpe, 2000). Our gold colour identifies *cones as a group*, without assigning classes or simulating colour vision.

## Sampling: More detail is not always more information

Now imagine projecting fine stripes onto a row of these receptors. As the stripes become too fine for the row to distinguish, different patterns can produce the same samples. This ambiguity is **aliasing**, where detail can be misrepresented rather than simply disappearing into blur.

We can isolate this effect by setting the anatomy aside and using equally spaced **point samples in one dimension**. Here frequency means cycles across the panel, not per degree of visual angle, and $N$ samples give a Nyquist boundary of $N/2$ cycles per interval. At the boundary, phase can make the signal disappear from the samples, so unique reconstruction requires appropriate band-limiting below it.

Start with 16 samples and increase the stripes beyond 8 cycles to reveal a lower-frequency pattern in the bottom row that agrees at every sample position. Adding samples moves the boundary, while **Shift ¼ cycle** changes where the stripes fall relative to the receptors. At certain settings, every sample lands on the same grey.

Real receptors have finite collecting areas, and ocular optics filter the pattern before neural circuitry, adaptation and eye movements also affect vision. Although human foveal aliasing has been demonstrated under carefully controlled conditions (Williams, 1985), our regular one-dimensional row only isolates the sampling ambiguity. It does not simulate retinal images or perception, or calculate someone's visual acuity.

## The retina is already doing computation

Sampling is only the beginning of the retinal response. When a rod or cone absorbs a photon, it initiates a biochemical cascade that **hyperpolarises** the cell and reduces glutamate release, starting a neural message rather than producing a tiny electrical photograph.

Bipolar cells carry signals towards ganglion cells while horizontal and amacrine cells contribute lateral interactions and other processing. Along the way, ON and OFF pathways respond differently to light increments and decrements, before ganglion-cell axons carry action potentials onwards through the optic nerve. This circuitry has multiple cell types and parallel routes rather than a single cone-to-wire connection (Kolb, *How the Retina Works*).

This also connects to the pupil reflex from Part 2: melanopsin-expressing **ipRGCs** are ganglion cells that respond intrinsically to light as well as receiving rod/cone input. The retina therefore contributes to the reflex through more than one kind of photoreceptor.

Our meshes locate some of these structures without implementing their phototransduction, receptive fields or spike trains. That would require another level of modelling, beyond what an animated arrow can explain.

## Put the receiving surface behind the optics

With the receiving tissue in place, we can position the cup behind the cornea, iris and lens from the earlier articles. Aligning their coordinate systems gives the following dimensions for the assembled, relaxed model, in millimetres:

| Measurement | Endpoints in this model | Value |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Central corneal thickness (CCT) | Anterior to posterior cornea, on axis | 0.55 |
| Internal anterior chamber depth (ACD) | Posterior cornea to anterior lens | 3.35 |
| Lens thickness (LT) | Anterior to posterior lens | 4.00 |
| Axial length (AXL) | Anterior cornea to the boundary representing the RPE | 24.30 |

The outer envelope measures approximately **24.4 mm wide and high** and **25.4 mm from the corneal apex to the outer posterior sclera**, extending beyond the RPE endpoint used for AXL in the table. A 10.8 mm retinal radius and centre at $z=-7.6\ \text{mm}$ place it in the anterior segment's coordinate system. The anterior inner wall follows the ciliary body's attachment profile, while the coats share boundaries and the retina tapers to a schematic ora serrata behind the ciliary body.

These **chosen dimensions** retain the anterior parts from Part 3 without fitting their shapes and spacing, including ciliary-muscle thickness, to a common biometric dataset. They therefore describe neither a measured person nor a validated schematic optical eye. Following the common optical-biometry convention, our AXL runs to the RPE (Ostrin et al., 2023), from the corneal apex at $z=5.6\ \text{mm}$ to the boundary at $z=-18.70\ \text{mm}$. The inner retina lies at a different distance, and **internal** ACD excludes corneal thickness.

Around the retina and RPE, the vascular **choroid** supplies the outer retina, including its photoreceptors, while retinal circulation supplies much of the inner retina (Kolb). Beyond the choroid sits the **sclera**, an opaque, collagen-rich coat that supports the globe mechanically and meets the cornea at the limbus (Watson & Young, 2004).

To leave this enclosure, ganglion-cell axons converge at the **optic disc**, nasal to the fovea, and pass through the scleral canal as the **optic nerve**. Since the disc has no rods or cones, it forms the anatomical basis of the blind spot. Our nerve must therefore leave beside the fovea rather than emerging from its centre.

We construct nested walls with a shared, offset opening for a short nerve segment. Each wall joins inner and outer surfaces with edges, with triangle winding facing out of the tissue: into the vitreous on the inner surface and away from it outside. The nerve matches the canal's axis and opening radius before bending beyond the sclera. Caching both closed and sectioned meshes, including cut faces, lets us switch views without rebuilding geometry.

We mark the **macula** around the fovea with a soft, darker orange-brown patch, using the common clinical description of a roughly $5.5\ \text{mm}$ diameter, whose boundaries depend on the definition (Webvision, *The Architecture of the Human Fovea*). A shallow Gaussian depression shapes the **foveal pit** in the inner surface while leaving the outer RPE boundary fixed. Its $0.10\ \text{mm}$ depth is a model parameter rather than a universal measurement, and the pit appears subtle at whole-eye scale compared with the earlier magnified layer view.

The major **retinal vessels** curve from the disc above and below the central macula, avoiding the foveal centre (Kolb; Provis et al., 2013). We sketch these routes as curves and build small tubes along the retinal surface, keeping their arrangement and visible calibres stylised without modelling the capillary network or exact foveal avascular-zone boundary.

We now have the main anatomical pieces in one scene, though predicting a retinal image would still require us to couple and validate their surfaces, refractive indices and distances. Predicting vision would go further, modelling how the retina and brain use that image as well.

For now, the construction gives light a surface to reach and cells to sample it, showing why adding more polygons does not necessarily add more visual information.
