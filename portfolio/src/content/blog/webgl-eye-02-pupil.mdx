In [Part 1](/blog/webgl-eye-01-cornea) we shaped the cornea, the fixed front lens of the eye. But a camera needs more than a lens: it needs an aperture, an adjustable hole that controls how much light gets in. In the eye that hole is the **pupil**, and the colored ring around it, the **iris**, is the muscle that opens and closes it.

> *Renaissance women used drops of deadly nightshade (Atropa belladonna, "beautiful lady") to widen their pupils for cosmetic effect, often not knowing the plant was quietly poisoning them.*

The pupil is not just a passive hole. It reacts to light in a fraction of a second, it trades sharpness against brightness, and it never sits perfectly still. In this part we model the iris, work out how pupil size sets the light reaching the retina, and simulate the reflex that drives it.

## The Iris: A living aperture

The iris is what gives eyes their color, but to an optical engineer it is a muscle that changes the pupil between about $2\ \text{mm}$ wide (in bright light) and $8\ \text{mm}$ wide (in the dark). It does this with two opposing muscles:

1. **The sphincter** is a ring of muscle around the pupil edge. When it contracts it makes the pupil smaller (**constriction**). It runs on the parasympathetic "rest and digest" nervous system.
2. **The dilator** is a set of myoepithelial cells arranged like spokes, running outward from the pupil. When it contracts it pulls the pupil open (**dilation**). It runs on the sympathetic "fight or flight" system.

The front of the iris is split into two rings by a wavy ridge called the **collarette**: an inner *pupillary zone* around the pupil and an outer *ciliary zone* out toward the white of the eye. It is dotted with pits and folds called **Fuchs crypts**, **Wölfflin nodules** in the periphery, and the **pupillary ruff** at the margin, along with concentric **contraction furrows** in the outer zone. These anatomical features are what give every iris its unique, mesmerizing texture.

To capture this biological complexity in our simulation, the model below builds the iris in 3D under a glassy cornea (although strictly speaking, the pupil is often slightly eccentric). The iris itself is drawn in a shader whose recipe is essentially two circles: throw away everything outside the iris, paint the hole in the middle black, and fill the ring between them with procedural noise to mimic the crypts and muscle fibers.

```glsl
float dist = distance(uv, center);   // distance from the pupil center
if (dist > irisRadius) discard;      // outside the iris: nothing to draw
if (dist < pupilRadius) {            // inside the pupil: the dark hole
  gl_FragColor = vec4(0.01, 0.01, 0.02, 1.0);
  return;
}
// in between: the colored iris, textured with layered noise (fbm)
```

Drag the diameter slider to open and close the pupil, turn on the muscle map to see the red sphincter ring and the blue radial dilator fibers, and switch the pigment to see how melanin changes the color. Drag the view to orbit it.

## Calculating light

Why does pupil size matter so much? Because the amount of light reaching the retina depends on the *area* of the hole. Small changes in pupil size have a big effect: going from a $2\ \text{mm}$ pupil in sunlight to an $8\ \text{mm}$ pupil in the dark is a 4x change in diameter, but a 16x change in area. However, area is not the whole story. The **Stiles-Crawford effect of the first kind (SCE-I)** shows that light entering through the *edge* of a wide pupil is less effective than light entering through the center, so the *effective* pupil area grows more slowly than the geometric area. This happens because the retina is not a flat, uniform sensor array; its cones act like tiny fiber optics pointed toward the pupil center. The Stiles-Crawford effect (Stiles & Crawford, 1933) is primarily associated with cone-mediated vision, so its influence is much less relevant under fully scotopic, rod-dominated conditions.

Beyond just letting light in, the pupil acts as the eye's aperture for sharpness. The optical quality of the eye is governed by its Point Spread Function (PSF) and Modulation Transfer Function (MTF), both of which are tightly coupled to pupil diameter. If the pupil is too small, diffraction blurs the image; if it is too large, peripheral optical aberrations from the cornea and lens ruin the focus. Pupil diameters around 3 to 4 mm often offer a useful balance between diffraction and aberrations, but the optimum depends on the eye and the visual task; resting size is not fixed.

> [!NOTE] 
> **Does iris color affect how much light enters?** 
>
> Yes. Iris pigmentation affects intraocular light scatter. Dark brown irises are packed with light-absorbing melanin. Lighter irises (like blue, green, or hazel) have less melanin, which can allow more light to scatter through iris tissue. This can contribute to straylight and glare, but iris color alone does not predict an individual's glare sensitivity (van den Berg et al., 1991).

How big should the pupil be at a given brightness? A useful rule of thumb gives a steady-state pupil diameter following a smooth curve of scene brightness, in the spirit of Moon and Spencer (1944). (For this visualisation we use a simplified luminance-only approximation inspired by established pupil-size models. Watson & Yellott's (2012) unified model additionally accounts for factors such as age, field size and binocular viewing. Remember that these describe *steady-state* adaptation, not the transient reflex).

$$d(L) = 4.9 - 3.0 \tanh\left(0.4 \log_{10}(L) + 0.4\right)$$

This gives an illustrative diameter in millimeters across dim to bright scenes, rather than an individual prediction. In code it is a one-liner:

```ts
function pupilFromLuminance(L) {                 // L = scene luminance (cd/m²)
  const d = 4.9 - 3.0 * Math.tanh(0.4 * Math.log10(Math.max(0.0001, L)) + 0.4);
  return Math.min(8, Math.max(2, d));       // clamp to 2-8 mm
}
```

In the model below you set the pupil size and the scene brightness by hand and read off the light reaching the retina: the geometric pupil area, the photopic retinal illuminance in trolands (Troland, 1917), and the **SCE-weighted effective area**. We use a simplified, centered Gaussian efficiency profile, $\eta(r) = e^{-\rho r^2}$ with $\rho = 0.085\ \text{mm}^{-2}$ as a model parameter. Weighting changes the estimated visual effectiveness of the light, not the physical pupil size; the unweighted troland value remains luminance times geometric pupil area.

## The light reflex

We know how the pupil size affects incoming light, but how does the body actually regulate this size to control light entering? Shine a light in someone's eye and their pupil shrinks. That is the **pupillary light reflex** (PLR). It is a simple but critical feedback loop. Light information driving the pupillary light reflex comes from rods, cones, and melanopsin-expressing ipRGCs. Rods and cones provide rapid responses, while melanopsin provides a slower, sustained contribution to pupil control. The signal shoots up the optic nerve. Retinal signals reach the pretectal nuclei through the optic nerves and tracts, with partial crossing at the optic chiasm. Pretectal neurons project to both Edinger-Westphal nuclei, with crossed connections through the posterior commissure. This drives the consensual light reflex in the opposite eye. Finally, the Edinger-Westphal output travels with cranial nerve III to the ciliary ganglion, then postganglionic fibres travel through the short ciliary nerves to the sphincter pupillae. Constrict, then relax.

Because this neural circuit runs deep through the brainstem, it acts as a diagnostic window into the brain. If a patient has suffered a traumatic brain injury, a doctor will shine a light in their eyes; a pupil that remains fixed and dilated can indicate severe pressure on the brainstem. The reflex is also heavily influenced by drugs, which is why opiates cause pinpoint pupils and some stimulants can produce pupil dilation.

The loop is a feedback controller, and like any real controller it is not instant. The neural pathway of this reflex is a 3D circuit that crosses the brainstem:

Three things shape how the pupil actually moves:

- **A delay.** We approximate the neural latency of the pupillary light reflex as $220\ \text{ms}$ before the pupil begins responding. This latency varies with stimulus conditions, luminance, adaptation state, and age.
- **Asymmetry.** Constriction is generally faster than redilation. For this visualisation we choose time constants of $0.12\ \text{s}$ and $0.65\ \text{s}$ respectively; these are simulation parameters, not universal physiological constants.
- **Pupillary unrest.** Even under perfectly steady light the pupil quivers slightly. This small spontaneous fluctuation in pupil size is often called hippus and can occur physiologically.

The delay and the asymmetry give the reflex its character. For the visualisation, we approximate the more complex PLR dynamics (like Stark's triple lag (Stark, 1959)) with asymmetric first-order responses. Each frame, the pupil eases toward the target diameter it was told to reach $220\ \text{ms}$ ago, using a simple first-order response that moves faster when closing than when opening:

```ts
const diff = targetDiameter - currentDiameter;
const tau = diff < 0 ? 0.12 : 0.65;   // fast to constrict, slow to dilate
currentDiameter += diff * (1 - Math.exp(-dt / tau)); // stable first-order update
```

The model below pairs the pupil with an oscilloscope. The dashed line is the target the reflex is chasing; the solid line is where the pupil actually is. ## Beyond light: Cognitive load and pupillometry

While the light reflex is the strongest driver of the pupil, it is not the only one. The pupil's size is a continuous tug-of-war between two opposing nervous systems:

| System | Muscle | Action | Common Triggers | Drug Effects |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Parasympathetic** ("Rest & Digest") | Sphincter | **Constricts** (Miosis) | Bright light, focusing on near objects, relaxation | Opioids commonly cause miosis through central autonomic pathways. |
| **Sympathetic** ("Fight or Flight") | Dilator | **Dilates** (Mydriasis) | Darkness, stress, arousal, cognitive load, adrenaline | Some stimulants can produce pupil dilation. |

Pupils often dilate during demanding cognitive tasks. Both sympathetic activity and reduced parasympathetic drive can contribute, with response size and timing depending on arousal, visual conditions, baseline pupil size and task structure (Beatty, 1982).

UI designers, psychologists, and researchers use eye trackers to study pupil responses associated with cognitive demand. While some have proposed using pupillometry to monitor **workplace fatigue** or **cognitive overload** in high-stakes environments like surgical theaters or air traffic control, the reality is incredibly messy. The pupil light reflex is overwhelmingly stronger than cognitive responses. Separating tiny, task-evoked dilations (often just a fraction of a millimeter) from the massive fluctuations caused by ambient lighting changes, blinks, and screen glare in a real-world environment is exceptionally difficult. In controlled laboratory settings, however, by keeping lighting strictly constant and establishing a rigorous baseline, researchers can still reliably measure these subtle markers of mental effort. Despite these hurdles, advances in machine learning and robust signal processing are opening exciting new avenues, giving researchers optimism that reliable real-world cognitive monitoring may soon be within reach.

The pupil also changes size when you focus on something close to your face. This is called the **near triad**: your eyes converge (turn inward), your lenses accommodate (bulge to focus), and your pupils constrict. The neural pathway for this bypasses the pretectal nucleus, traveling from the visual cortex to the supraoculomotor area. Constricting the pupil when looking at something close increases your depth of field, exactly like stopping down a macro lens on a camera.

## Real-Time Pupil Size with Neon

Since we are talking about pupils, why just simulate them when you can drive the 3D model with your actual eyes? If you have access to a pair of [Pupil Labs Neon](https://pupil-labs.com/products/neon) eye-tracking glasses, you can hook them up directly to this blog post!

Since the Neon Companion app bundles the Monitor app, we can fetch it and embed it in a hidden iframe whose lifetime we control. By quietly reading the gaze payload from its data stream, we have everything we need without complex integrations.

In the [documented Neon eye-state gaze payload](https://pupil-labs.github.io/pl-realtime-api/dev/guides/under-the-hood/#decoding-gaze-data), the left and right pupil diameters are big-endian Float32s at byte offsets 9 and 37. We locate the payload after the RTP header, validate both eyes, and plot the available measurements. The graph keeps the last 1000 samples; missing-eye values appear as gaps. The measurement stream can operate at 200 Hz, depending on the Companion device and conditions, while rendering follows the browser's animation frames.

Make sure your computer or iPad is on the same Wi-Fi network as your eye tracker, enable **Compute Eye State** in Companion, enter its address, and click Connect. The browser will intercept the binary stream, parse the structs on the fly, and drive the 3D iris from the mean of the valid pupil measurements, using one eye when the other is unavailable.

> [!WARNING]
> This experimental browser/WebGL demo fetches and patches the Companion Monitor bundle on your local network. The pupil packet format is documented, but bundle interception is intentionally fragile: Monitor updates or browser networking and security policies can break it. For production integrations, use Pupil Labs’ [official Real-Time API and client](https://pupil-labs.github.io/pl-realtime-api/).
