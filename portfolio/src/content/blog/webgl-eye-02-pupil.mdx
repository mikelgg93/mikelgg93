In [Part 1](/blog/webgl-eye-01-cornea) we shaped the cornea, the fixed front lens of the eye. But a camera needs more than a lens: it needs an aperture, an adjustable hole that controls how much light gets in. In the eye that hole is the **pupil** (which is actually slightly eccentric, being nasally and inferiorly displaced), and the colored ring around it, the **iris**, is the muscle that opens and closes it.

> *Renaissance women used drops of deadly nightshade (Atropa belladonna, "beautiful lady") to widen their pupils for cosmetic effect, often not knowing the plant was quietly poisoning them.*

The pupil is not just a passive hole. It reacts to light in a fraction of a second, it trades sharpness against brightness, and it never sits perfectly still. In this part we model the iris, work out how pupil size sets the light reaching the retina, and simulate the reflex that drives it.

## The Iris: A living aperture

The iris is what gives eyes their color, but to an optical engineer it is a muscle that changes the pupil between about $2\ \text{mm}$ wide (in bright light) and $8\ \text{mm}$ wide (in the dark). It does this with two opposing muscles:

1. **The sphincter** is a ring of muscle around the pupil edge. When it contracts it makes the pupil smaller (**constriction**). It runs on the parasympathetic "rest and digest" nervous system.
2. **The dilator** is a set of myoepithelial cells arranged like spokes, running outward from the pupil. When it contracts it pulls the pupil open (**dilation**). It runs on the sympathetic "fight or flight" system.

The front of the iris is split into two rings by a wavy ridge called the **collarette**: an inner *pupillary zone* around the pupil and an outer *ciliary zone* out toward the white of the eye. It is dotted with pits and folds called **Fuchs crypts**, **Wolffian nodules** near the pupillary margin, and concentric **contraction furrows** in the outer zone, all of which give every iris its unique texture.

The model below builds the iris in 3D under a glassy cornea. The iris itself is drawn in a shader whose recipe is essentially two circles: throw away everything outside the iris, paint the hole in the middle black, and fill the ring between them with procedural noise for the crypts and fibers.

```glsl
float dist = distance(uv, center);   // distance from the pupil center
if (dist > irisRadius) discard;      // outside the iris: nothing to draw
if (dist < pupilRadius) {            // inside the pupil: the dark hole
  gl_FragColor = vec4(0.01, 0.01, 0.02, 1.0);
  return;
}
// in between: the colored iris, textured with layered noise (fbm)
```

## Calculating light

Why does pupil size matter so much? Because the amount of light reaching the retina depends on the *area* of the hole. Small changes in pupil size have a big effect: going from a $2\ \text{mm}$ pupil in sunlight to an $8\ \text{mm}$ pupil in the dark is a 4x change in diameter, but a 16x change in area. However, area is not the whole story. The **Stiles-Crawford effect of the first kind (SCE-I)** shows that light entering through the *edge* of a wide pupil is less effective than light entering through the center, so the *effective* pupil area grows more slowly than the geometric area.

How big should the pupil be at a given brightness? A useful rule of thumb often derived from Moon and Spencer (1944) gives a resting, binocular steady-state pupil diameter following a smooth curve of scene brightness. (For a more modern and comprehensive model that accounts for age and field size, one would use the Watson and Yellott (2012) formula, and remember that these describe *steady-state* adaptation, not the transient reflex).

$$d(L) = 4.9 - 3.0 \tanh\left(0.4 \log_{10}(L) - 0.2\right)$$

This gives a diameter in millimeters across the full range from starlight to bright sun. In code it is a one-liner:

```ts
function pupilDiameter(L) {                 // L = scene luminance (cd/m²)
  const d = 4.9 - 3.0 * Math.tanh(0.4 * Math.log10(L) - 0.2);
  return Math.min(8, Math.max(2, d));       // clamp to 2-8 mm
}
```

In the model below you set the pupil size and the scene brightness by hand and read off the light reaching the retina: the geometric pupil area, the retinal illuminance in trolands, and how much the Stiles-Crawford falloff trims it once the pupil is wide.

## The light reflex

We know how the pupil size affects incoming light, but how does the body actually regulate this size to control light entering? Shine a light in someone's eye and their pupil shrinks. That is the **pupillary light reflex** (PLR). It is a simple but critical feedback loop: light is detected by intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs) using the photopigment melanopsin, the signal travels up the optic nerve, projects bilaterally to both the ipsilateral and contralateral pretectal nuclei, and then to the Edinger-Westphal nuclei in the brainstem (driving the consensual light reflex in the opposite eye), and comes back down via the ciliary ganglion to drive the sphincter muscle. Constrict, then relax.

Because this neural circuit runs deep through the brainstem, it acts as a diagnostic window into the brain. If a patient has suffered a traumatic brain injury, a doctor will shine a light in their eyes; a pupil that remains fixed and dilated can indicate severe pressure on the brainstem. The reflex is also heavily influenced by drugs, which is why opiates cause pinpoint pupils and stimulants cause massive dilation.

The loop is a feedback controller, and like any real controller it is not instant. The neural pathway of this reflex is a fascinating 3D circuit that crosses the brainstem:

Three things shape how the pupil actually moves:

- **A delay.** It takes about $220\ \text{ms}$ for the signal to make the round trip before the muscle even starts moving.
- **Asymmetry.** Constriction is fast (a time constant near $0.12\ \text{s}$); dilation is slow (nearer $0.65\ \text{s}$). Your pupil snaps shut but opens back up lazily.
- **Pupillary unrest.** Even under perfectly steady light the pupil quivers slightly, a low, constant wobble of a few tenths of a millimeter (often loosely called hippus, though true hippus is pathological).

The delay and the asymmetry give the reflex its character. While a true physiological model involves the complex non-linear dynamics of Lawrence Stark's control systems (Stark, 1959), for visual rendering purposes we can approximate it with a few lines of code. Each frame, the pupil eases toward the target diameter it was told to reach $220\ \text{ms}$ ago, using a simple exponential decay that moves faster when closing than when opening:

```ts
const diff = targetDiameter - currentDiameter;
const tau = diff < 0 ? 0.12 : 0.65;   // fast to constrict, slow to dilate
currentDiameter += (diff / tau) * dt; // ease toward the target this frame
```

The model below pairs the pupil with an oscilloscope. The dashed line is the target the reflex is chasing; the solid line is where the pupil actually is. Switch the light from dark to bright and watch the lag and the asymmetry play out. Turn the latency off to see how twitchy the pupil would be without its built-in delay.

## Beyond light: Cognitive load and pupillometry

While the light reflex is the strongest driver of the pupil, it is not the only one. The pupil's size is a continuous tug-of-war between two opposing nervous systems:

| System | Muscle | Action | Common Triggers | Drug Effects |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Parasympathetic** ("Rest & Digest") | Sphincter | **Constricts** (Miosis) | Bright light, focusing on near objects, relaxation | Opiates and heroin disinhibit the Edinger-Westphal nucleus via GABAergic interneurons, causing extreme "pinpoint" pupils. |
| **Sympathetic** ("Fight or Flight") | Dilator | **Dilates** (Mydriasis) | Darkness, stress, arousal, cognitive load, adrenaline | Stimulants (MDMA, cocaine, amphetamines) or psychedelics cause massive, wide-open dilation. |

Because the dilator muscle is wired directly to the sympathetic nervous system, your pupils expand slightly when you are stressed, aroused, or thinking hard. If you give someone a complex math problem to solve in their head, their pupils will dilate, peaking at the moment of highest cognitive load, and shrink back down the moment they solve it.

UI designers, psychologists, and researchers use eye trackers to measure this invisible "cognitive load". In studies of workers, measuring pupil size is critical for assessing **workplace fatigue**, **cognitive overload**, and **stress during high-stakes tasks**. For instance, air traffic controllers, surgeons, or heavy machinery operators can be monitored using pupillometry to detect when they are reaching dangerous levels of mental saturation. This translates directly to real-world ergonomics and safety, allowing systems to adapt or alert users before critical errors occur due to mental exhaustion. When analyzing pupil size for these purposes, one typically has to take into account that the pupil light reflex is much stronger than cognitive responses. You must ensure lighting conditions remain strictly constant, remove blinks and artifacts, and establish a baseline pupil size before measuring the tiny task-evoked dilations (often just a fraction of a millimeter).

The pupil also changes size when you focus on something close to your face. This is called the **near triad**: your eyes converge (turn inward), your lenses accommodate (bulge to focus), and your pupils constrict. Constricting the pupil when looking at something close increases your depth of field, exactly like stopping down a macro lens on a camera.
