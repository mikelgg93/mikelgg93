In [Part 1](/blog/webgl-eye-01-cornea) we shaped the cornea, and in [Part 2](/blog/webgl-eye-02-pupil) we added a living aperture. Light can now enter our model, but there is still a problem: focusing on a mountain requires a different adjustment from reading the words on your phone.

A camera often solves this by moving its lenses, whereas the eye mainly changes the shape of the **crystalline lens**, which sits behind the iris. This mechanism, called **accommodation**, relies on a ring of muscle around the lens and has a surprising feature: focusing up close involves releasing some of the pull that keeps the lens flattened.

We'll start with a deliberately crude shape, give it an optical interior, then add the machinery that moves it. Finally, we'll look at how we measure a real lens and what changes when it loses transparency or flexibility.

## First approximation: Two opposing domes

We already built a dome in Part 1 and used $Q$ to change its asphericity. Make a second dome, turn it around and bring the rims together: our first **biconvex lens**.

The front of a relaxed lens is flatter than the back. We borrow central radii of **10.2 mm anterior** and **6 mm posterior** from the [unaccommodated Navarro schematic eye (1985)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4032096/). These are reference-model values, not universal anatomy. With $Q=0$ and a 9 mm rim diameter, this first mesh borrows Navarro's radii without reproducing its aspheric prescription.

```ts
const front = new THREE.Mesh(createTeachingCap(10.2), frontMaterial);
const back = new THREE.Mesh(createTeachingCap(6), backMaterial);
back.rotation.y = Math.PI;
// Both rims lie at z = 0: the two domes meet here.
```

The domes slowly separate and rejoin. Rotate the view and follow the grid: their rims meet, but their slopes do not, leaving a sharp ridge between two separate meshes.

## Round the equator and add a material

A real lens has a rounded **equator**, where its front and back meet. Smoothing normals would hide our ridge without changing the silhouette, so we need to reshape the profile.

Keep the central spherical patches and join them to the equator with a short [**cubic Bézier curve**](https://pomax.github.io/bezierinfo/#cubic). Its control handles set the end tangents: one follows the dome; the other follows the optical axis, bringing both halves to a common tangent at the equator. Revolving the profile gives one closed surface. Three.js provides [`CubicBezierCurve`](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/CubicBezierCurve.html) for this step.

```ts
const blend = new THREE.CubicBezierCurve(
  domeEdge,
  domeEdge.clone().add(new THREE.Vector2(dr, dr * slope)),
  new THREE.Vector2(equatorRadius, handleHeight),
  new THREE.Vector2(equatorRadius, 0),
);
// Sample both halves into one profile, then revolve it.
const normalizedProfile = profile.map(
  p => new THREE.Vector2(p.x / 4.5, p.y / 2),
);
const geometry = new THREE.LatheGeometry(normalizedProfile, 96);
geometry.rotateX(Math.PI / 2); // align the optical axis with z
```

We set a **4 mm central thickness**, another relaxed Navarro reference value, and keep the illustrative 9 mm diameter. The blend handles and thickness split are drawing choices, giving a smooth approximation rather than a fit to a measured peripheral lens.

The profile is in millimetres; dividing its radial and axial coordinates by 4.5 and 2 prepares it for the scale below. Over the same unfilled grid, we reveal a transmitting material with low roughness, a faint warm tint and procedural reflections. Only the material changes, not the shape.

```ts
const lens = new THREE.Mesh(geometry, clearLensMaterial());
lens.scale.set(4.5, 4.5, 2); // unit mesh → 9 mm wide, 4 mm thick
```

Inside `clearLensMaterial()`, Three.js supplies the physical-material shader. An approximate relative index of $1.40/1.336$ suggests lens tissue immersed in ocular fluid, while an elliptical chord estimate shortens the transmission path toward the equator. Together with tint and roughness, these settings control appearance; they do not calculate the lens's internal optical gradient.

## Give the lens an interior: GRIN

An elastic **capsule** encloses the lens. Inside, elongated fibre cells form the outer **cortex** and older central **nucleus**—a region of the lens, not a cell nucleus. New fibres are added throughout life. The lens has no blood vessels, and mature fibres lose their nuclei and other organelles. Along with the organisation of densely packed crystallin proteins, this reduces the microscopic index variations that would scatter light (Bassnett et al., 2011).

Yet the index is not uniform: it generally rises from cortex to centre, where light travels more slowly. This **gradient refractive index**, or **GRIN**, contributes to optical power and aberrations alongside surface curvature. MRI measurements and MRI-derived index estimates indicate that both shape and index distribution change with accommodation (Khan et al., 2018).

As we saw in Part 1, internal ocular optics can partially compensate corneal aberrations in young eyes (Artal et al., 2001). That balance involves the whole eye; strengthening an isolated lens gradient does not automatically improve the retinal image.

A smooth index gradient steers light, whereas irregular microscopic variations can scatter it. At a surface, the ray changes direction according to Snell's law; within a gradient, it bends continuously. The next demo isolates this difference using a quadratic profile:

$$n(x,y) = n_{\mathrm{edge}} + \Delta n\left(1 - \frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2}\right)$$

Here $x$ is the optical axis, $y$ is the height above it, and $a$ and $b$ are the axial and equatorial semiaxes. The formula applies inside the elliptical section. We choose $a=2\ \text{mm}$, $b=4.5\ \text{mm}$ and $n_{\mathrm{edge}}=1.37$ as model parameters. At $\Delta n=0.05$, the central index is $1.42$. The surrounding medium is assigned $1.336$, approximating the aqueous and vitreous for this single-wavelength example. This is a teaching profile, not a fit to an individual's GRIN.

Reproducing a published eye would require its surfaces, index profiles, distances and accommodation rules to work together and be validated together; an optical prescription alone does not supply muscle forces or cataract scattering.

## The ciliary muscle: Pull less to focus closer

To move our lens, we need the **ciliary body** and its smooth **ciliary muscle**. The muscle has a roughly wedge-shaped section, with outer **longitudinal**, intermediate **radial or reticular**, and inner **circular** regions. Their fibres interweave as parts of one muscle.

On the inner body, the **ciliary processes** are vascular, epithelial folds involved in aqueous humour production, not radial muscle fibres. Between this apparatus and the capsule, fine **zonules** attach to anterior, equatorial and posterior lens regions (Bassnett, 2021).

In the classical Helmholtz account of accommodation:

1. **For distance**, the ciliary muscle relaxes. Zonular tension holds the lens in a relatively flatter configuration.
2. **For near**, the muscle contracts and the ciliary ring narrows. The main lens-flattening zonular load decreases, allowing the lens and capsule to adopt a rounder configuration.
3. The lens becomes thicker centrally and its equatorial diameter decreases. Its optical power increases.

The lens contains no focusing muscle, and the zonules remain attached during near focus. Their real force distribution is more complex than our radial spokes, but the essential relationship is visible: the ring contracts while the lens rounds up. Imaging studies measure both changes during accommodation (Richdale et al., 2013).

We reuse the rounded 9 mm × 4 mm lens, narrowing it as it thickens to preserve mesh volume. The zonules attach to its surface and the iris follows its anterior contour. This profile also serves the cataract and final demos; only the separate GRIN diagram keeps its simpler elliptical section.

With the iris and outer wall removed, we can see the ciliary body and its inward-facing brown processes. Yellow zonules pass between the folds and fan onto the capsule, represented here by the lens surface. A divider reveals a colour map of the interwoven muscle regions within the anterior wedge.

Watch the view alternate between a distant landscape and a nearby book: the inner ciliary tissue moves inward and slightly forward as the lens rounds up. The bottom-right readout is the **modelled response** driving that deformation, not a measurement of mesh power. Rotate or pause the model to inspect the connections, and drag the divider to compare muscle regions with the tissue surface.

We allocate geometry once, then deform the muscle and update fibre buffers during animation. A first-order easing step smooths the transition:

```ts
const tau = 0.25; // seconds: an animation parameter
shownAccommodation += (targetAccommodation - shownAccommodation)
  * (1 - Math.exp(-dt / tau));
```

That quarter-second value controls the animation, not a universal physiological response time. The demo also respects the browser's reduced-motion preference.

## How OCT measures lens shape

**Optical coherence tomography (OCT)** lets us move from a plausible mesh toward measured shape. Interference between returning light and a reference beam resolves depth; scanning across the eye builds a cross-section, or **B-scan**. Anterior-segment systems image lens surfaces through the pupil, within the area accessible to the instrument.

In [Pérez-Merino et al. (2015)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4679276/), OCT volumes reconstruct both lens surfaces during accommodation. Figure 3(a) compares relaxed and accommodated scans; Figure 3(b) shows departures from fitted spheres in the central $4\ \text{mm}$ of the relaxed eye, revealing asymmetries our rotational mesh omits.

Recovering physical shape requires refractive-index assumptions and distortion correction: OCT measures optical path length, and the beam has already refracted through overlying structures. Image brightness describes returned signal, not a direct GRIN or transparency map. MRI provides complementary information about the lens and ciliary apparatus.

## From distance to dioptres

A closer object sends more divergent rays toward the eye, requiring more power to focus them on the retina. For an eye relaxed and focused at infinity, the additional demand is approximately the reciprocal of object distance in metres:

$$A_{\mathrm{demand}} \approx \frac{1}{d}$$

A target at $1\ \text{m}$ asks for about $1\ \text{D}$; at $50\ \text{cm}$, $2\ \text{D}$; at $25\ \text{cm}$, $4\ \text{D}$. This **demand** is the stimulus; the **response** is what the eye supplies, which may lag behind it depending on viewing conditions.

We represent the whole eye with one **reduced-eye** element, using Emsley's conventional distance power $F_0 = 60\ \text{D}$ and image-space index $n' = 4/3$ (Harris, 2009). This combines cornea and lens into an equivalent element; it neither assigns $60\ \text{D}$ to the lens nor simply adds the powers of two separated lenses.

In the paraxial approximation, the outgoing vergence is the incoming vergence plus the optical power. With a near demand $A_{\mathrm{demand}}$ and response $A_{\mathrm{response}}$, the image distance is:

$$l' = \frac{n'}{F_0 + A_{\mathrm{response}} - A_{\mathrm{demand}}}$$

Distances start at the equivalent principal plane, with the retina fixed $n'/F_0 \approx 22.2\ \text{mm}$ behind it. This reduced-eye distance is not anatomical axial length. When response matches demand, focus stays on the retina.

## Who tells the muscle to move?

Accommodation is mainly driven by parasympathetic output. Preganglionic fibres from the Edinger-Westphal complex travel with **cranial nerve III** to the **ciliary ganglion**. Postganglionic fibres then reach the ciliary muscle through the **short ciliary nerves** (McDougal & Gamlin, 2015).

The pathway is shared with the pupil circuit from Part 2, but its targets have different jobs: the iris sphincter adjusts the aperture, while the ciliary muscle changes the load on the lens.

Looking near usually combines accommodation with **convergence** and **pupil constriction**: the near response. A smaller pupil can increase depth of focus, but cannot restore lens flexibility. Our focus diagrams isolate accommodation; the final assembly adds the accompanying pupil response.

## Cataracts: Losing transparency

Changes to lens proteins and fibre organisation alter how light is scattered and absorbed. A **cataract**, or lens opacity, can reduce contrast and sharpness, cause glare, and make colours appear faded or yellowed.

Its location shapes the opacity: **nuclear** cataracts affect the centre and may yellow or brown; **cortical** opacities often form peripheral spokes; **posterior subcapsular** opacities lie just in front of the posterior capsule. They can coexist rather than following a fixed sequence (Aliancy & Mamalis, 2017).

When cataract disrupts daily activities enough to warrant surgery, the cloudy lens is removed and usually replaced by an artificial intraocular lens (National Eye Institute).

> **Before replacement lenses**
>
> Claims that cataract surgery began in pharaonic Egypt remain disputed (Blomstedt, 2014). Ancient **couching** displaced the cloudy lens from the visual axis, clearing an obstruction but removing its focusing contribution and carrying serious risks. Later extraction removed the lens; modern surgery usually replaces its optical role. Harold Ridley's first intraocular lens implantation in 1949 marked a key step toward that approach (Leffler et al., 2020).

## Presbyopia: When the lens cannot keep up

With age, declining accommodation makes sustained near work difficult: **presbyopia**. It can occur while the lens remains clear, so it is distinct from cataract even when both are present.

Ciliary contraction can persist in older eyes (Strenk et al., 1999). The lens's changing mechanical properties play a major role, alongside continued growth and surrounding geometry; donor-lens experiments show a marked age-related loss of the ability to change optical power (Glasser & Campbell, 1998).

We can explore the consequence by limiting the response to an **available accommodation**:

```ts
const response = Math.min(demand, availableAccommodation);
const unmetDemand = demand - response;
```

Start at $25\ \text{cm}$ with only $2\ \text{D}$ available. The target demands $4\ \text{D}$, so the model falls short by $2\ \text{D}$ and the focus lies behind the retina. Move the target out to $50\ \text{cm}$ and the demand fits within the available range again.

## Bring the three parts together

We can now bring our three parts into one scene: the cornea provides the front refracting surface, the iris sets the opening, and the lens changes shape behind it.

The aqueous-filled **anterior chamber** lies between posterior cornea and iris, with the lens behind the pupil. It communicates with the posterior chamber through that opening. At its edges, cornea continues into sclera at the limbus and the iris root joins the ciliary body near the chamber angle. Our cutaway removes these boundaries for visibility; the intact chamber is enclosed (AAO EyeWiki, *Gonioscopy*).

As the target moves near, the lens thickens and pupil constricts; both relax when it returns to distance. This illustrates the near response at constant lighting, with illustrative pupil ranges and timing. Convergence needs a second eye and is omitted. Rotate or pause the view to inspect how the structures meet.

We have now explored three mechanisms: a fixed front surface, an adjustable aperture and a lens that changes focus. In the real eye, they work together to form the retinal image.
