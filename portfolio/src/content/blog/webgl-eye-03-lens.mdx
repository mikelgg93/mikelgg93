In [Part 1](/blog/webgl-eye-01-cornea) we shaped the cornea, and in [Part 2](/blog/webgl-eye-02-pupil) we added a living aperture. Light can now enter our model, but there is still a problem: focusing on a mountain requires a different adjustment from reading the words on your phone.

A camera often solves this by moving its lenses, whereas the eye mainly changes the shape of the **crystalline lens**, which sits behind the iris. This mechanism, called **accommodation**, relies on a ring of muscle around the lens and has a surprising feature: focusing up close involves releasing some of the pull that keeps the lens flattened.

We'll build it from a simple shape, add an optical interior and then the machinery that moves it, before exploring how a real lens is measured and what happens when it loses transparency or flexibility.

## First approximation: Two opposing domes

The dome we built in Part 1 gives us a starting point. There we used $Q$ to change its asphericity; here we can make a second dome, turn it around and bring the rims together to form our first **biconvex lens**.

Because the front of a relaxed lens is flatter than the back, we give the two domes different central radii: **10.2 mm anterior** and **6 mm posterior**, borrowed from the [unaccommodated Navarro schematic eye (1985)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4032096/). These reference values set the proportions rather than describing universal anatomy. We keep $Q=0$ and a 9 mm rim diameter for now, so we are using Navarro's radii without reproducing its aspheric prescription.

```ts
const front = new THREE.Mesh(createTeachingCap(10.2), frontMaterial);
const back = new THREE.Mesh(createTeachingCap(6), backMaterial);
back.rotation.y = Math.PI;
// Both rims lie at z = 0: the two domes meet here.
```

As the domes slowly separate and rejoin, rotate the view and follow the grid around their edges. The rims meet, but their slopes do not, leaving a sharp ridge between two separate meshes.

## Round the equator and add a material

A real lens has a rounded **equator** where its front and back meet, so that ridge is our next problem. Smoothing the normals would soften its appearance without changing the silhouette, so we need to reshape the profile itself.

We keep the central spherical patches and join each to the equator with a short [**cubic Bézier curve**](https://pomax.github.io/bezierinfo/#cubic), using Three.js's [`CubicBezierCurve`](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/CubicBezierCurve.html). One control handle follows the dome's tangent while the other follows the optical axis, bringing both halves to a common tangent at the equator. We can then revolve the combined profile to make one closed surface.

```ts
const blend = new THREE.CubicBezierCurve(
  domeEdge,
  domeEdge.clone().add(new THREE.Vector2(dr, dr * slope)),
  new THREE.Vector2(equatorRadius, handleHeight),
  new THREE.Vector2(equatorRadius, 0),
);
// Sample both halves into one profile, then revolve it.
const normalizedProfile = profile.map(
  p => new THREE.Vector2(p.x / 4.5, p.y / 2),
);
const geometry = new THREE.LatheGeometry(normalizedProfile, 96);
geometry.rotateX(Math.PI / 2); // align the optical axis with z
```

We give the joined lens a **4 mm central thickness**, another relaxed Navarro value, and retain the illustrative 9 mm diameter. The blend handles and thickness split remain drawing choices, so this gives us a smooth approximation rather than a fit to a measured peripheral lens.

Dividing the profile's radial and axial coordinates in millimetres by 4.5 and 2 prepares it for the scale below. We can then reveal a transmitting material over the unfilled grid, keeping the shape fixed while adding low roughness, a faint warm tint and procedural reflections.

```ts
const lens = new THREE.Mesh(geometry, clearLensMaterial());
lens.scale.set(4.5, 4.5, 2); // unit mesh → 9 mm wide, 4 mm thick
```

Inside `clearLensMaterial()`, Three.js's physical-material shader uses an approximate relative index of $1.40/1.336$ to suggest lens tissue immersed in ocular fluid. We also shorten the transmission path toward the equator with an elliptical chord estimate. Together with tint and roughness, these choices give the lens its appearance, but they do not calculate its internal optical gradient.

## Give the lens an interior: GRIN

Inside its elastic **capsule**, the lens contains elongated fibre cells arranged into an outer **cortex** and an older central **nucleus**. Here, nucleus means a region of the lens rather than a cell nucleus. New fibres are added throughout life, while mature fibres lose their nuclei and other organelles. In this tissue, which has no blood vessels, that organisation and the arrangement of densely packed crystallin proteins reduce microscopic index variations that would scatter light (Bassnett et al., 2011).

Transparency does not mean the index is uniform: it generally rises from cortex to centre, where light travels more slowly. This **gradient refractive index**, or **GRIN**, contributes to optical power and aberrations alongside surface curvature, and MRI measurements and MRI-derived index estimates indicate that both shape and index distribution change with accommodation (Khan et al., 2018).

This connects back to Part 1, where internal ocular optics could partially compensate corneal aberrations in young eyes (Artal et al., 2001). Because that balance involves the whole eye, strengthening the gradient of an isolated lens does not automatically improve the retinal image.

Following a ray shows how the gradient contributes: it changes direction according to Snell's law at a surface, then bends continuously inside. This smooth steering differs from scattering by irregular microscopic variations, and the next demo isolates it with a quadratic index profile:

$$n(x,y) = n_{\mathrm{edge}} + \Delta n\left(1 - \frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2}\right)$$

Here $x$ follows the optical axis, $y$ is the height above it, and $a$ and $b$ are the axial and equatorial semiaxes of the elliptical section where the formula applies. We choose $a=2\ \text{mm}$, $b=4.5\ \text{mm}$ and $n_{\mathrm{edge}}=1.37$, giving a central index of $1.42$ when $\Delta n=0.05$. For the surrounding medium we use $1.336$, approximating the aqueous and vitreous in this single-wavelength example. These parameters define a teaching profile rather than a fit to an individual's GRIN.

Our demos separate mesh construction, ray bending and accommodation. Reproducing a published eye would require its surfaces, indices, distances and accommodation rules to be validated together. An optical prescription still would not supply muscle forces or cataract scattering, so we now turn to the structures that move the lens.

## The ciliary muscle: Pull less to focus closer

The **ciliary body** surrounds the lens and contains the smooth **ciliary muscle** that drives accommodation. In cross-section, this muscle forms a rough wedge with outer **longitudinal**, intermediate **radial or reticular**, and inner **circular** regions whose fibres interweave as parts of one muscle.

Along the body's inner surface, the **ciliary processes** form vascular, epithelial folds involved in aqueous humour production. These are distinct from the muscle fibres. The fine **zonules** connect the ciliary apparatus to the capsule, attaching across anterior, equatorial and posterior lens regions (Bassnett, 2021).

In the classical Helmholtz account of accommodation:

1. **For distance**, the ciliary muscle relaxes while zonular tension holds the lens in a relatively flatter configuration.
2. **For near**, the muscle contracts and the ciliary ring narrows, reducing the main lens-flattening zonular load so the lens and capsule can adopt a rounder configuration.
3. As the lens becomes thicker centrally and its equatorial diameter decreases, its optical power increases.

The lens thus changes shape without a focusing muscle of its own, while the zonules remain attached. Their real force distribution is more complex than our radial spokes, but the model preserves the essential relationship: the ring contracts while the lens rounds up. Imaging studies measure both changes during accommodation (Richdale et al., 2013).

To show that movement, we reuse our rounded 9 mm × 4 mm lens and narrow it as it thickens, preserving the mesh volume. The zonules attach to this surface while the iris follows its anterior contour. The cataract comparison and final assembly also use this profile, while the separate GRIN diagram retains its simpler ellipse.

Removing the iris and outer wall exposes the ciliary body, its inward-facing brown processes and the yellow zonules passing between them. The zonules fan onto the capsule, represented here by the lens surface, while the divider reveals a colour map of the interwoven muscle regions within the anterior wedge.

As the view alternates between a distant landscape and a nearby book, watch the inner ciliary tissue move inward and slightly forward as the lens rounds up. The bottom-right readout gives the **modelled response** driving this deformation, rather than measuring the mesh's optical power. Rotate or pause to inspect the connections, and drag the divider to reveal the muscle regions.

In code, we allocate the geometry once and animate it by deforming the muscle and updating the existing fibre buffers. A first-order easing step keeps the transition smooth:

```ts
const tau = 0.25; // seconds: an animation parameter
shownAccommodation += (targetAccommodation - shownAccommodation)
  * (1 - Math.exp(-dt / tau));
```

The quarter-second value sets the pace of this animation rather than a universal physiological response time, and the demo respects the browser's reduced-motion preference.

## How OCT measures lens shape

So far, we have chosen a plausible shape. **Optical coherence tomography (OCT)** gives us a way to compare it with measurements, using interference between returning light and a reference beam to resolve depth. Scanning across the eye produces a cross-section, or **B-scan**, and anterior-segment systems can image the lens surfaces through the area of the pupil accessible to the instrument.

For example, [Pérez-Merino et al. (2015)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4679276/) used OCT volumes to reconstruct both lens surfaces during accommodation. Figure 3(a) compares relaxed and accommodated scans, while Figure 3(b) shows departures from fitted spheres in the central $4\ \text{mm}$ of the relaxed eye. Those departures reveal asymmetries that our surface of revolution leaves out.

Recovering the physical shape takes more than reading distances from the scan, because OCT measures optical path length and the beam has already refracted through the structures in front. The reconstruction therefore needs refractive-index assumptions and distortion correction. Image brightness describes the returned signal rather than a direct GRIN or transparency map, with MRI providing complementary information about the lens and ciliary apparatus.

## From distance to dioptres

These shape changes help focus the more divergent rays arriving from a closer object, which require more optical power to focus on the retina. Starting from an eye relaxed and focused at infinity, the additional demand is approximately the reciprocal of object distance in metres:

$$A_{\mathrm{demand}} \approx \frac{1}{d}$$

A target at $1\ \text{m}$ therefore asks for about $1\ \text{D}$, rising to $2\ \text{D}$ at $50\ \text{cm}$ and $4\ \text{D}$ at $25\ \text{cm}$. These distances describe the **demand**, or stimulus, while the **response** is what the eye actually supplies and may fall short depending on viewing conditions.

To follow the focus without tracing every surface, we represent the whole eye with one **reduced-eye** element. We use Emsley's conventional distance power $F_0 = 60\ \text{D}$ and image-space index $n' = 4/3$ (Harris, 2009). The equivalent element represents cornea and lens together, rather than assigning $60\ \text{D}$ to the lens or simply adding the powers of two separated lenses.

In the paraxial approximation, the outgoing vergence is the incoming vergence plus the optical power. With a near demand $A_{\mathrm{demand}}$ and response $A_{\mathrm{response}}$, the image distance is:

$$l' = \frac{n'}{F_0 + A_{\mathrm{response}} - A_{\mathrm{demand}}}$$

We measure distances from the equivalent principal plane and fix the retina $n'/F_0 \approx 22.2\ \text{mm}$ behind it. Although this reduced-eye distance is not anatomical axial length, it lets us see the relationship clearly: when response matches demand, the focus stays on the retina.

## Who tells the muscle to move?

For the ciliary muscle to make this adjustment, it receives mainly parasympathetic input. Preganglionic fibres from the Edinger-Westphal complex travel with **cranial nerve III** to the **ciliary ganglion**, where the pathway continues through postganglionic fibres in the **short ciliary nerves** to the muscle (McDougal & Gamlin, 2015).

This route also serves the pupil circuit from Part 2, where the iris sphincter adjusts the aperture. Although the ciliary muscle instead changes the load on the lens, the two often act together when we look near.

Together with **convergence**, accommodation and **pupil constriction** form the near response. The smaller pupil can increase depth of focus, although it cannot restore lens flexibility. Our focus diagrams isolate accommodation to make its contribution clear, and the final assembly will add the accompanying pupil response.

## Cataracts: Losing transparency

Focusing depends on transparency as well as shape. When lens proteins and fibre organisation change, they alter how light is scattered and absorbed, and a **cataract**, or lens opacity, can reduce contrast and sharpness, cause glare and make colours appear faded or yellowed.

The pattern depends on where the opacity develops. **Nuclear** cataracts affect the centre and may yellow or brown, whereas **cortical** opacities often form peripheral spokes. **Posterior subcapsular** opacities lie just in front of the posterior capsule, and all three patterns can coexist rather than following a fixed sequence (Aliancy & Mamalis, 2017).

What we see from outside is only part of the story: backscattered light reveals an opacity to an observer, while forward scatter affects the retinal image. These are not interchangeable measurements (Crnej et al., 2017), and the cloudiness shader here illustrates appearance without calculating multiple scattering, glare or a person's view through the lens.

When cataract disrupts daily activities enough to warrant surgery, the cloudy lens is removed and usually replaced by an artificial intraocular lens (National Eye Institute).

> **Before replacement lenses**
>
> Claims that cataract surgery began in pharaonic Egypt remain disputed (Blomstedt, 2014). Among historical treatments, **couching** displaced the cloudy lens from the visual axis, clearing the obstruction but removing its focusing contribution and carrying serious risks. Later extraction removed the lens itself, while modern surgery usually replaces its optical role with an implant. Harold Ridley's first intraocular lens implantation in 1949 marked a key step toward that approach (Leffler et al., 2020).

## Presbyopia: When the lens cannot keep up

A lens can also remain clear while losing the ability to change focus. As accommodation declines with age, sustained near work becomes difficult: this is **presbyopia**, a different change from cataract even though both can be present.

That loss of accommodation does not simply mean the muscle has stopped working, since ciliary contraction can persist in older eyes (Strenk et al., 1999). Changes in the lens's mechanical properties play a major role, alongside its continued growth and the surrounding geometry. Donor-lens experiments show a marked age-related loss of the ability to change optical power (Glasser & Campbell, 1998).

We can explore what this means for focus by returning to our reduced eye and limiting its response to an **available accommodation**:

```ts
const response = Math.min(demand, availableAccommodation);
const unmetDemand = demand - response;
```

Start with a target at $25\ \text{cm}$ and only $2\ \text{D}$ available. Because the target demands $4\ \text{D}$, the model falls short by $2\ \text{D}$ and the focus lies behind the retina. As you move it out to $50\ \text{cm}$, the demand falls back within the available range.

## Bring the three parts together

We can now bring the three parts into one scene: the cornea provides the front refracting surface, the iris sets the opening, and the lens changes shape behind it.

The aqueous-filled **anterior chamber** lies between the posterior cornea and iris, communicating through the pupil with the posterior chamber, while the lens sits behind the pupil. Around its edges, the cornea continues into the sclera at the limbus and the iris root joins the ciliary body near the chamber angle. We remove part of these boundaries to see inside, but the chamber is enclosed in the intact eye (AAO EyeWiki, *Gonioscopy*).

Watch the lens thicken and the pupil constrict as the target moves near, then relax again as it returns to distance. The sequence illustrates the near response at constant lighting, using illustrative pupil ranges and timing, though convergence would need a second eye and is omitted.
