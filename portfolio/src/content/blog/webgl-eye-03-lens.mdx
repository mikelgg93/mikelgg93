In [Part 1](/blog/webgl-eye-01-cornea) we shaped the cornea. In [Part 2](/blog/webgl-eye-02-pupil) we gave the eye a living aperture. Light can now enter our model, but there is still a problem: a mountain and the words on your phone do not ask the eye to focus in the same way.

A camera often solves this by moving its lenses. The eye mainly changes the shape of one: the **crystalline lens**, tucked behind the iris. The mechanism is called **accommodation**, and its motor is a ring of muscle around the lens. The surprising part is that focusing up close involves releasing some of the pull on the lens.

We'll start with a deliberately crude shape, give it an optical interior, then add the machinery that moves it. Finally, we'll look at how we measure a real lens and what changes when it loses transparency or flexibility.

## First approximation: Two opposing domes

In [Part 1](/blog/webgl-eye-01-cornea), we already built a dome and used $Q$ to change its asphericity. We can reuse that construction here: make a second dome, turn it around, and bring the rims together. There is our first **biconvex lens**.

The front of a relaxed lens is flatter than the back. We fix the central radii at **10.2 mm anterior** and **6 mm posterior**, taking their magnitudes from the unaccommodated Navarro schematic eye (Navarro et al., 1985). These are reference-model values, not universal anatomy. For this first mesh we keep $Q=0$ and a 9 mm rim diameter; we are borrowing the radii, not reproducing Navarro's aspheric prescription.

```ts
const front = new THREE.Mesh(createTeachingCap(10.2), frontMaterial);
const back = new THREE.Mesh(createTeachingCap(6), backMaterial);
back.rotation.y = Math.PI;
// Both rims lie at z = 0: the two domes meet here.
```

The domes start joined, then slowly separate and come together again. Rotate the view and follow the construction grid. The rims meet, but their slopes do not: the seam is a sharp ridge. The two meshes also remain separate objects. Joining their positions is only the first construction step.

## Round the equator and add a material

A real lens has a rounded **equator**, where its front and back meet. Smoothing the normals alone would disguise our ridge without changing its silhouette. We need to change the profile itself.

Keep the central spherical patches, stop them before the edge, and use a short **cubic Bézier curve** to turn each patch into the equator. The first control handle follows the dome's tangent. The last handle points along the optical axis, so the front and back arrive at the equator with a common tangent. Revolving this continuous profile gives us one closed lens surface.

```ts
const blend = new THREE.CubicBezierCurve(
  domeEdge,
  domeEdge.clone().add(new THREE.Vector2(dr, dr * slope)),
  new THREE.Vector2(equatorRadius, handleHeight),
  new THREE.Vector2(equatorRadius, 0),
);
// Sample both halves into one profile, then revolve it.
const normalizedProfile = profile.map(
  p => new THREE.Vector2(p.x / 4.5, p.y / 2),
);
const geometry = new THREE.LatheGeometry(normalizedProfile, 96);
geometry.rotateX(Math.PI / 2); // align the optical axis with z
```

We now set a **4 mm central thickness**, also a relaxed Navarro reference value, while keeping the illustrative 9 mm diameter. The blend handles and the anterior/posterior thickness split are drawing choices. This is a smooth anatomical approximation, not a fitted peripheral lens shape. The construction grid below makes that curved edge easier to inspect.

The profile above is in millimetres; dividing its radial and axial coordinates by 4.5 and 2 prepares it for the scale below. Keep exactly the same mesh. Now reveal a transmitting material over the unfilled construction grid: low roughness, a faint warm tint, and reflections from our procedural lighting. The shape is unchanged; only its response to light changes.

```ts
const lens = new THREE.Mesh(geometry, clearLensMaterial());
lens.scale.set(4.5, 4.5, 2); // unit mesh → 9 mm wide, 4 mm thick
```

Inside `clearLensMaterial()`, Three.js supplies the physical-material shader. We use an approximate relative index of $1.40/1.336$ to suggest lens tissue immersed in ocular fluid. A small shader adjustment shortens the transmission path toward the equator, using an elliptical chord approximation. Tint, roughness and this path estimate control the appearance; they do not reproduce the full optical behaviour of the lens or its internal gradient.

## Give the lens an interior: GRIN

The crystalline lens is a biconvex body enclosed by an elastic **capsule**. Inside it, elongated fibre cells form the outer **cortex** and the older central **nucleus**. Here “nucleus” names the central lens region, not a cell nucleus. New fibres are added throughout life. The lens has no blood vessels; its transparency depends on specialised cellular organisation, including the loss of nuclei and other organelles from mature fibres and the arrangement of densely packed crystallin proteins. These features reduce the small-scale refractive-index variations that would scatter light (Bassnett et al., 2011).

It is not a piece of glass with one uniform refractive index, either. The index generally increases from the outer cortex toward the central region. A higher refractive index means light travels more slowly through that part of the lens. This **gradient refractive index**, or **GRIN**, contributes to the lens's optical power and aberrations, alongside the curvature of its surfaces. MRI measurements and MRI-derived estimates of refractive index indicate that both lens shape and the index distribution change with accommodation (Khan et al., 2018).

There is a link back to Part 1, too: measurements in young eyes show that internal ocular optics can partially compensate corneal aberrations (Artal et al., 2001). This balance involves the whole eye; increasing the gradient in an isolated lens does not automatically improve retinal image quality.

A smooth change in index across the lens steers light; irregular microscopic variations can scatter it. At a sharp interface, a ray changes direction according to Snell's law. Inside a smoothly varying index, its direction changes continuously. To show the difference, the next demo traces rays through an isolated lens with an illustrative quadratic profile:

We choose $a=2\ \text{mm}$, $b=4.5\ \text{mm}$ and $n_{\mathrm{edge}}=1.37$ as model parameters. At $\Delta n=0.05$, the central index is $1.42$. The surrounding medium is assigned $1.336$, approximating the aqueous and vitreous for this single-wavelength example. This is a teaching profile, not a fit to an individual's GRIN.

These are deliberately separate demonstrations, not implementations of the full named models. ## The ciliary muscle: Pull less to focus closer

Around the lens sits the **ciliary body**, containing the smooth **ciliary muscle**. Its cross-section is roughly wedge-shaped, with an outer **longitudinal** portion, an intermediate **radial or reticular** portion, and an inner **circular** portion. These fibre systems interweave; they are regions of one muscle, not three independent motors.

The inward-facing **ciliary processes** are vascular, epithelial folds of the ciliary body, involved in aqueous humour production. They are not radial muscle fibres. The **zonules** form a fine supporting network between the ciliary apparatus and the capsule, with attachments on the anterior, equatorial and posterior regions of the lens (Bassnett, 2021).

In the classical Helmholtz account of accommodation:

1. **For distance**, the ciliary muscle relaxes. Zonular tension holds the lens in a relatively flatter configuration.
2. **For near**, the muscle contracts and the ciliary ring narrows. The main lens-flattening zonular load decreases, allowing the lens and capsule to adopt a rounder configuration.
3. The lens becomes thicker centrally and its equatorial diameter decreases. Its optical power increases.

The lens itself contains no focusing muscle. Nor do its supporting fibres detach when you look near. The real zonular network has several attachment regions and a more complex distribution of forces than our radial spokes can show. The useful idea is the change in load: the ring contracts, while the lens rounds up. Imaging studies measure these accompanying changes in the lens and ciliary muscle (Richdale et al., 2013).

For the moving anatomy below, we switch from the 9 mm × 4 mm construction lens to an asymmetric ellipsoidal mesh, initially 9.2 mm wide and 3.6 mm thick. This simpler deforming surface keeps volume constant as it changes shape; it is a separate teaching model, not a continuation of the same fitted lens.

We now add the ciliary body. A continuous, rounded muscle carries subtle bundle detail; the optional colour map locates its interwoven regions. const tau = 0.25; // seconds: an animation parameter


That quarter-second value controls the animation, not a universal physiological response time. The demo also respects the browser's reduced-motion preference.

## How OCT measures lens shape

How do we get beyond an attractive mesh? **Optical coherence tomography**, or **OCT**, uses interference between returning light and a reference beam to resolve tissue structure in depth. Scanning across the eye builds a cross-section, often called a **B-scan**. Anterior-segment systems can image lens surfaces through the pupil, with the visible area depending on the instrument and acquisition.

In [Pérez-Merino et al. (2015)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4679276/), researchers used OCT volumes to reconstruct the anterior and posterior lens surfaces during accommodation. Figure 3(a) in the linked paper compares relaxed and accommodated scans; Figure 3(b) maps departures from fitted spherical surfaces in the central $4\ \text{mm}$ of the relaxed eye. Those asymmetries show what our rotationally symmetric mesh leaves out.

There is an optical catch. OCT depth is based on optical path length, and the beam has already been refracted by the structures in front of the surface being measured. Reconstructing physical shape therefore requires refractive-index assumptions and distortion correction. Image brightness mainly describes the returned signal; it is not a direct GRIN map, a muscle-force measurement or a simple scale of visual transparency. MRI and other methods provide complementary information about the lens and ciliary apparatus.

## From distance to dioptres

The closer an object gets, the more its rays diverge before reaching the eye. To bring them back onto the retina, the eye needs more optical power.

For an eye focused at infinity with accommodation relaxed, the approximate additional demand is the reciprocal of object distance in metres:

$$A_{\mathrm{demand}} \approx \frac{1}{d}$$

A target at $1\ \text{m}$ asks for about $1\ \text{D}$; at $50\ \text{cm}$, $2\ \text{D}$; at $25\ \text{cm}$, $4\ \text{D}$. **Demand** describes the stimulus. **Response** describes what the eye actually supplies. Real responses can lag behind demand and depend on viewing conditions; they need not match this simple reciprocal exactly.

To make the optical bookkeeping visible, we now replace the entire eye with one **reduced-eye** element. We choose the conventional distance power $F_0 = 60\ \text{D}$ and image-space index $n' = 4/3$, as in Emsley's reduced eye (Harris, 2009). The equivalent element represents cornea and lens together. We are not assigning $60\ \text{D}$ to the crystalline lens, or simply adding the separate powers of two thick, separated lenses.

In the paraxial approximation, the outgoing vergence is the incoming vergence plus the optical power. With a near demand $A_{\mathrm{demand}}$ and response $A_{\mathrm{response}}$, the image distance is:

$$l' = \frac{n'}{F_0 + A_{\mathrm{response}} - A_{\mathrm{demand}}}$$

Distances here are measured from the model's equivalent principal plane. We keep the retina at $n'/F_0 \approx 22.2\ \text{mm}$ behind that plane; this is a reduced-eye distance, not a claim about the anatomical length of every eye. When response matches demand, the focus stays on the retina.

## Who tells the muscle to move?

Accommodation is mainly driven by parasympathetic output. Preganglionic fibres from the Edinger-Westphal complex travel with **cranial nerve III** to the **ciliary ganglion**. Postganglionic fibres then reach the ciliary muscle through the **short ciliary nerves** (McDougal & Gamlin, 2015).

This shares an outgoing route with the pupil pathway from Part 2, but the muscles do different jobs. The iris sphincter adjusts the aperture; the ciliary muscle changes the loading of the lens.

When we shift our gaze toward a near target, accommodation usually accompanies **convergence** of the eyes and **pupil constriction**: the near response. A smaller pupil can increase depth of focus, but it does not restore lost lens flexibility. The focus experiment below isolates accommodation; the final assembly then adds the accompanying pupil response.

## Cataracts: Losing transparency

The transparent lens depends on its microscopic organisation. When that organisation changes, including changes to lens proteins and fibre structure, light can be scattered and absorbed differently. A **cataract** is an opacity of the lens. It can reduce contrast, cause glare and make colours appear faded or yellowed, as well as reduce sharpness.

The location matters. **Nuclear** cataracts affect the central region and may yellow or brown. **Cortical** opacities often form peripheral spokes or wedges. **Posterior subcapsular** opacities sit just in front of the posterior capsule. These patterns can coexist; they are not three mandatory stages of one sequence (Aliancy & Mamalis, 2017).

Compare the clear lens with all three opacity patterns at once. They share the same rounded geometry, lighting and viewing angle. Drag to turn all four together and inspect where the cloudiness lies.

> **Before replacement lenses**
>
> Did cataract surgery begin in ancient Egypt? Claims about pharaonic Egypt remain disputed; the surviving evidence does not establish that origin (Blomstedt, 2014). Ancient **couching** displaced the cloudy lens out of the visual axis. It could clear an obstruction, but left the eye without the lens's focusing power and carried serious risks. Later extraction removed the lens; modern surgery usually replaces its optical role with an implant. Harold Ridley's first intraocular lens implantation in 1949 was a key step toward that approach (Leffler et al., 2020).

## Presbyopia: When the lens cannot keep up

Accommodation declines with age, eventually making sustained near work difficult without help: **presbyopia**. A lens can remain optically clear while losing accommodative range; cataract and presbyopia describe different changes, although both can be present.

It is tempting to blame a tired focusing muscle, but measurements show that ciliary contraction can persist in older eyes (Strenk et al., 1999). Age-related changes in the lens's mechanical properties play a major role; its continuing growth and the geometry of the surrounding apparatus also matter. Experiments on donor lenses demonstrate a marked loss of the ability to change optical power with age (Glasser & Campbell, 1998).

We can explore the optical consequence without pretending to calculate someone's age. Give the model an **available accommodation** and let its response stop at that limit:

```ts
const response = Math.min(demand, availableAccommodation);
const unmetDemand = demand - response;
```

Start at $25\ \text{cm}$ with only $2\ \text{D}$ available. The target demands $4\ \text{D}$, so the model falls short by $2\ \text{D}$ and the focus lies behind the retina. Move the target out to $50\ \text{cm}$ and the demand fits within the available range again.

## Bring the three parts together

The cornea supplies the front refracting surface. The iris sets the opening, and the crystalline lens changes shape behind it. Here they finally share one scene.

The aqueous-filled **anterior chamber** lies between the posterior cornea and the iris, with the lens behind the pupil. It communicates with the posterior chamber through the pupil. The cornea continues into the sclera at the limbus, and the iris root joins the ciliary body near the chamber angle. A cutaway removes part of those boundaries to show the space; it does not mean that the chamber is open in an intact eye (AAO EyeWiki, *Gonioscopy*).

The eye stays open in section. As attention shifts toward the nearby object, the lens thickens and the pupil constricts; as it returns to distance, both relax toward their starting state. This illustrates the near response at constant lighting. The pupil range and timing are model parameters, not a universal relationship between dioptres and pupil diameter. Convergence would require a second eye and is not shown. Rotate the view or pause the animation to inspect the connections.
