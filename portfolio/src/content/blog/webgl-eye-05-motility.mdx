Our eye can admit light, change focus and give the image a receiving surface. But the model still stays pointed in one direction. To look from the horizon to a word on the page, the whole globe must turn inside the orbit.

That brings us to the **extraocular muscles**. In [Part III](/blog/webgl-eye-03-lens/), the ciliary muscle changed the lens's shape; here, striated muscles outside the eye change its orientation. We will reuse the cornea, iris, relaxed lens and the coats from <PublishedPost id="webgl-eye-04-retina" fallback="Part IV"><a href="/blog/webgl-eye-04-retina/">Part IV</a></PublishedPost>, place their centre of rotation, and then build the surrounding paths.

## First, decide what rotates

Rotating each part separately would quickly pull the construction apart. Instead, put the whole eye inside a group, with its pivot at our chosen globe centre, $z=-7.6\ \text{mm}$. The cornea, retina, iris and lens then share one rigid transformation. This pivot is a construction choice, not a measurement of the eye's physiological centre of rotation.

```ts
const pivot = new THREE.Group();
pivot.position.z = CUP_CENTRE_Z;
eye.position.z = -CUP_CENTRE_Z;
pivot.add(eye);
```

The second translation expresses the existing geometry relative to that pivot. We can now turn the eye without changing its central corneal thickness or moving the lens independently of the sclera. The orbital attachment points, however, must stay outside this rotating group. We omit the external optic-nerve segment here: it would need its own deforming path rather than rotating rigidly with the globe.

For this schematic **right eye**, positive x points nasally, towards the nose, positive y is superior and positive z is anterior. Anterior z is also the model's starting gaze axis; we do not fit a person's visual-axis offset.

## Four recti and two obliques

Six muscles rotate the globe: **medial, lateral, superior and inferior rectus**, plus **superior and inferior oblique**. The levator palpebrae superioris is another extraocular muscle, but it raises the eyelid, so it is outside this globe-rotation model (StatPearls, *Eye Extraocular Muscles*).

The recti run forwards from the orbital apex to the anterior sclera. That apex lies nasally relative to the gaze axis: centring it behind the pupil would give the muscles the wrong pulling directions. We use coordinates from Filip et al.'s **UPAT v4** model, transformed into our axes. Insertion directions are preserved and projected onto the globe built in the earlier chapters. These are equivalent points of force application from a reference model; the visible tendon fans are not measured surgical footprints.

The obliques need different paths. The superior oblique travels towards a superomedial **trochlea**, where its tendon changes direction before reaching the superior globe behind the equator. The inferior oblique starts anteriorly on the medial orbital floor and passes beneath the globe towards its posterolateral attachment. Giving all six muscles the same posterior origin would miss this distinction.

## Give each route a belly and an insertion

A straight cylinder would pass through the globe. The mechanical path joins origin to pulley, then follows a tangent and an arc over a frictionless spherical envelope wherever it needs to wrap around the eye. Length and pulling direction come from that same path:

```ts
const path = musclePath(id, eyeRotation);
const torque = path.moment.clone().multiplyScalar(tensionN);
```

Around the curve, we draw elliptical sections with a different profile for each muscle. The recti have fuller, flattened bellies that continue into broad scleral tendons. The superior oblique narrows before the trochlea and spreads into a thin tendon after reflection; the inferior oblique retains a shorter fleshy arc beneath the globe. Subtle longitudinal striation suggests fibre direction without adding an external texture. The dimensions and striations are appearance parameters, not reconstructed muscle fascicles. In this exterior view, the relaxed lens and ciliary apparatus remain in the construction but are omitted from the draw pass; Part III provides their interior views.

The insertion belongs to the rotating globe. Its new world position follows the eye's quaternion, while the orbital guides remain fixed:

```ts
const worldInsertion = localInsertion.clone()
  .sub(globeCentre)
  .applyQuaternion(eyeRotation)
  .add(globeCentre);
```

We update vertex positions in the same buffers as the eye turns. A geometric clearance step moves curve samples out of the scleral shell if needed; this keeps the drawing legible without solving contact pressure or tissue deformation. At the insertion, the tendon fan follows scleral curvature rather than hovering above it as a flat strap.

## From paths to forces

Pulleys change the effective pulling direction; their role is supported by imaging and histology (Demer et al., 1995). Here we use UPAT's fixed-pulley approximation. A moving, compliant pulley, such as those studied by Wei et al. (2022), would require solving its forces and displacement too.

Each muscle's total tension combines an **active** contribution, dependent on activation and fibre length, with a **passive** contribution that increases with stretch. Maximum isometric force, optimal fibre length and tendon length come from UPAT. The force–length curves were evaluated with OpenSim and are interpolated in the browser. Tendons are treated as inextensible. We first solve static fixation, where shortening velocity is zero; during movement we also use the same model’s force–velocity curve.

```ts
const tension = activeForce + passiveForce;
const muscleTorque = momentArm.clone().multiplyScalar(tension);
```

The moment arm is the cross product of insertion position and pulling direction, expressed in metres. We sum all six muscle torques and the elastic resistance of the orbital tissues. The eye is in equilibrium when that sum approaches zero. For those tissues we use an isotropic rotation-vector adaptation of Filip et al.'s linear and cubic coefficients; this is not an identical reproduction of their model.

## Looking together, deviating differently

First, we calculate activations bounded between 0 and 1 that hold each eye at a distant fixation target. Recruitment starts from a chosen 8% coactivation level: a controller assumption. The reference orientation is compatible with Listing's law for our chosen primary position (Tweed & Vilis, 1987).

We then keep those commands and change one property of the right eye. Reducing lateral-rectus active force to 25% shifts the equilibrium inward. Superior-oblique weakness can produce elevation and excyclotorsion. Multiplying inferior-rectus passive resistance by four explores mechanical restriction. These factors are teaching perturbations, not clinical grades of paralysis or fibrosis.

The altered orientation is solved in **three dimensions**, including torsion; the affected eye is free to depart from the target orientation. The other eye maintains reference fixation. Choose a gaze direction to see how the imbalance changes.

H and V report right-eye equilibrium deviation from reference fixation, positive nasally and superiorly. Positive torsion is excyclotorsion, measured relative to the shortest-rotation orientation towards that gaze. The force readouts include active and passive tension; **Debug** exposes the components and residual torque. To move between fixations, we integrate muscle torque, globe inertia and orbital damping. Activation changes with a model time constant, and active tension also depends on shortening velocity. We use a homogeneous globe and UPAT coefficients, with internal steps of at most 2 ms and an implicit velocity solve to keep the integration stable. Reduced-motion mode shows the equilibrium directly.

## Muscle actions depend on eye position

Near primary position, the usual action summary is:

| Muscle | Actions | Motor nerve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Medial rectus | Adduction | III |
| Lateral rectus | Abduction | VI |
| Superior rectus | Elevation, intorsion, adduction | III |
| Inferior rectus | Depression, extorsion, adduction | III |
| Superior oblique | Intorsion, depression, abduction | IV |
| Inferior oblique | Extorsion, elevation, abduction | III |

These are anatomical actions, not commands calculated by the demo. For the vertical recti and obliques, the balance of vertical and torsional action depends on the eye's position. This helps explain why clinical examination uses different gaze directions to assess them: abduction emphasises vertical rectus action, while adduction emphasises the vertical actions of the obliques (StatPearls).

The motor pathways here also differ from the ciliary-ganglion route to the pupil discussed in [Part II](/blog/webgl-eye-02-pupil/). Cranial nerve III serves several targets, but they do not all share the same downstream pathway.

## What we can check

Tests check torque directions, agreement with muscle-length changes, local equilibrium stability and convergence in all nine gaze directions. The perturbations produce qualitatively expected signs. That validates parts of the calculation, but does not establish that the predicted angles agree with patients.

This model lets us separate active weakness from passive restriction. It does not yet include binocular fusion, neural adaptation, near convergence or vestibular responses. Studying a particular strabismus would require fitting anatomy and muscle properties, then comparing deviations with independent data. The dynamics converge to the same equilibria and have been checked at different rendering rates, but still need comparison with eye-movement recordings before their timing can be interpreted as physiological saccades.
